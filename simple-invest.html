Simple Invest

Mobile UX Design UI Design Multi-market

Simple Invest launched in Singapore as a self-serve investment product inside UOB's TMRW app. When it worked, the business decided to expand to Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia. The plan was lift and shift — same product, same flows, localised language and currency.

We knew that wouldn't hold. Three different regulatory environments, three different user bases, three different levels of digital and financial confidence. The question wasn't how to localise — it was which parts of the Singapore product were actually portable, and which were just Singapore.

My Role

Lead designer across all three markets. I drove the framework decision — what could be shared, what had to vary — and ran cross-market alignment across design, product, legal, and engineering.

Key Design Decisions

Before designing anything for the new markets, we audited what Singapore had actually built. That audit is where the real work started.

Decision 01

Audited what was universal before assuming anything was

Singapore users are unusually financially literate and digitally confident, even by regional standards. Simple Invest had been designed from that reference point — it just hadn't been named as a design assumption. Carrying it into three different markets without checking it was the core risk.

We ran an audit against one question: which parts of this product depend on the Singapore user? The navigation model, the visual hierarchy, the core interaction patterns — most of that was portable. What wasn't: the onboarding assumptions, the level of financial jargon in the copy, and the implicit expectation that users arrived already knowing what they wanted to do.

That distinction drove everything else. Market-agnostic components stayed shared. Journey-level flows varied wherever regulation or user context required it. One product, three implementations — but only after we'd been honest about what one product actually meant.

Decision 02

Indonesia broke two things — the screen and the seam

Indonesia surfaced the most visible problems. Currency values run fourteen to sixteen digits. The UI had been built around short, clean Singapore dollar amounts. Drop a Rupiah balance into that layout and numbers overflow, alignment breaks, and the interface that felt simple in Singapore reads as cluttered in Jakarta. That was the first problem. The second was underneath it.

The formatting fix was dynamic number display — abbreviating large values, surfacing 'B' for billions, restructuring how currency rendered so the hierarchy stayed readable regardless of digit count.

The second problem was structural. Part of the Indonesia journey had to route through an existing banking module with no design control on our side. In testing, users hit that transition and thought the app had crashed — one moment they were in our flow, the next they were dropped into a completely different interface with no warning. We couldn't redesign the module. We built a dedicated transition screen instead: something that told users exactly what was happening and why the interface was about to change. That turned a crash moment into a deliberate handoff.

Decision 03

Thailand's drop-off wasn't about length

Thailand had a regulatory suitability questionnaire we couldn't shorten — every question was mandated. It was longer than Singapore's. The natural assumption was that drop-off came from length.

When we looked at where drop-off actually clustered, it wasn't spread evenly across the form. It concentrated around specific questions that used financial regulatory terminology retail investors didn't recognise. The questionnaire wasn't too long. It was written for someone who already understood what the words meant.

We couldn't remove a single question. What we could do was rewrite how each was framed — plain language, legal meaning preserved. Drop-off improved without touching one regulatory requirement.

Outcome

Simple Invest launched across all three markets with a shared component foundation. The visual language stayed consistent. The flows varied where they had to.

Each market surfaced a different problem, but the pattern underneath was the same: we had built Simple Invest around a Singapore user — financially literate, digitally confident, already familiar with the product category. Those assumptions were invisible while we were inside Singapore. The moment we moved, they surfaced. Indonesia showed it in scale and formatting. Thailand showed it in language. We found that out market by market, when it would have been faster to find it at the start.
